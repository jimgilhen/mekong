The framework

How product specs and verification work with a co-packer

You approved a product. The risk in co-packing is that later runs slowly stop matching it: a little darker, a little thinner, a different cap, a label with a printing error. Nobody changed the recipe on purpose, and nobody can prove what the standard was, because it was never written down tightly enough to check. I call that drift.

I prevent it with three working documents: the Product Spec, the Packaging Specification, and the Verification Protocol. Your counsel attaches them to the agreement with your co-packer, so they are enforceable. I work from your approved finished product, not from your recipe. The recipe stays yours. Your own paperwork may call these documents an annex or an exhibit. What matters is that the content exists.

The four documents at a glance

Master Quality Agreement

The signed agreement between you and the co-packer, written by your counsel, that the other three documents attach to: consequences of failure, change control, per-lot test data, retained samples, traceability, recalls.

Drift prevented: nothing makes the plant answerable when a lot fails.

Product Spec

The food: color, °Brix, pH, water activity, salinity, alcohol by volume (ABV) where applicable, consistency, fill, visual, and taste and smell, as measurable ranges built from your approved product.

Drift prevented: the product slowly moving away from the one you approved.

Packaging Specification

The pack: seal and closure, label and printing, ingredient list and allergen statement as printed, barcode, lot and date code, carton, pallet.

Drift prevented: quiet changes in materials and pallets, and printing errors that reach the shelf.

Verification Protocol

How each production run is checked against the other two: what I measure on site, sampling plans, defect classes, what happens when a lot fails.

Drift prevented: the first two documents sitting on paper with nobody checking them.

Where my role ends

  • I don’t write, change, or review your recipe.
  • I don’t draft legal terms or give legal advice.
  • I don’t set food safety limits or certify food safety.
  • I don’t engineer or redesign packaging, or certify that it will survive transit.
  • I don’t release or reject the lot. You decide.

Product Spec: what goes in it

The baseline comes from your approved product. For color, water activity, pH, °Brix, salinity and, where applicable, ABV, an accredited lab takes every baseline reading, using the method defined for that product, so the standard rests on traceable numbers. I set the consistency and sensory baselines myself. Each parameter has a target value and a range, measured by a method written into the specification. Where a Codex Alimentarius standard exists for your category, I reference it when defining what the product is.

Initial ranges are time-boxed. For the first commercial lots (for example, the first three, or the first run if you produce less often), the ranges are outer limits and never loosen. After those lots they tighten by a method agreed in advance, such as recalculating from the first lots’ data with a maximum width. The tightened ranges take effect automatically, with no renegotiation. The target value stays fixed, and my reports track where each lot lands inside its range, so a plant drifting toward the loose edge is flagged even while lots still pass. Your counsel writes the automatic-tightening clause into the agreement.

Taste and smell are defined by a retained sealed reference sample and a short flavor profile written with you (aroma, main flavor notes, balance, texture, finish), built using ISO sensory analysis methods, plus a zero-tolerance list of named off-notes: burnt or scorched, metallic, rancid, fermented, bitter.

Packaging Specification: two ways to build it

Path 1: you have a packaging consultant

Your consultant writes the Packaging Specification and you approve it. I turn it into inspection checks and inspect against the consultant’s standard.

Path 2: you use the co-packer’s packaging

The plant states what it can do, and I record it as the Packaging Specification: seal, cap torque, carton strength, pallet pattern, artwork, barcode. I run a light document check, not an engineering review, covering food-contact materials, supplier test data for the carton and pallet, and whether your shipping route calls for a packaging consultant or a transit test. You approve it before it becomes the standard.

The contents cover the primary pack and seal, closure and cap torque, label and artwork (including print quality, smearing, misprints and color against the approved artwork), ingredient list and allergen statement as printed, net contents and claims as approved, barcode, lot and date code, master carton, and pallet pattern and wrap. I check that what is printed matches the artwork you approved. Whether the label is legally compliant is for your regulatory advisor. For a long or rough shipping route, I recommend a packaging consultant or a transit test, and your counsel should decide in the agreement who carries the risk if packaging fails in transit.

Verification Protocol: what I check and how

The lab takes the baseline readings once, when the Product Spec is set. At each inspection, my on-site readings are checked against those baseline ranges. My instrument calibration records are kept and available to you, and I check any instrument I don’t own before I accept its readings. Sampling uses AQL under ISO 2859-1 (US equivalent ANSI/ASQ Z1.4), with limits set by lot size and product risk.

How taste and smell are assessed

Taste and smell are assessed using sensory methods drawn from ISO 6658 (methodology) and ISO 5492 (vocabulary), as written into your Product Spec. Spoilage and contamination off-notes are reported immediately and the lot is held. A quality hold never rests on one person’s palate. If the panel can’t agree, the findings and the retained sample go to you.

If a reading is disputed

The first step is to measure the same sample side by side with the plant and compare calibration records. If it is still disputed, a retained split sample goes to a pre-agreed accredited lab, which measures it using the method named in the agreement. The binding terms, who pays for the lab, and the timing belong in the agreement your counsel writes. The plant keeps sealed retains of each lot, with the number and time set in the agreement. At each inspection I also take one tamper-sealed sample, log it, and hold it, so a dispute sample never rests only on the plant’s own storage.

How an engagement runs

  1. BaselineYou provide your approved product. An accredited lab takes baseline readings. I take the consistency baseline, write the flavor profile with you, retain the taste and smell reference, set the ranges, and define the method for each attribute. This produces the Product Spec.
  2. PackagingPath 1 (your consultant writes it) or Path 2 (the plant states its specs and I record them). You approve. This produces the Packaging Specification.
  3. ProtocolI write the Verification Protocol from the first two documents: instruments, on-site checks, AQL sampling, defect classes.
  4. AgreementYou and your counsel attach the three documents to the Master Quality Agreement.
  5. InspectI check each production run, scheduled or unannounced, and report with readings and photos. You decide to release, hold, rework, or reject.
  6. Review driftAfter the first lots the ranges tighten by the method agreed in advance. I also update the documents through change control.
