Questions to ask your co-packer before you sign

If you’re new to co-packing, one factory’s sales pitch can sound much like the next. These questions help you tell them apart and decide what to get in writing. Each comes with why it matters and what a good answer sounds like.

Ask for numbers, not a “yes.” A “yes” can mean “I heard you” or “I’ll try,” not “I agree.” Ask for the specific number or action, repeat it back in writing, and ask them to confirm it.

If you only ask five

  1. Will it run on a line suited to my product?
  2. Can I speak to a brand with a similar product?
  3. Will you sign our spec and initial each range?
  4. Can our inspector be on the floor?
  5. Is final payment tied to a passed inspection?

Can they make your product?

1What equipment will my product run on, and is it suited to my product’s thickness and pack format?Factories often say they can fill “any liquid or sauce.” A thick sauce pushed through a filler built for thin liquids drifts in fill weight and can ruin the run.A named machine and a line speed for your product, not “we can run anything.”
2Which brands making a similar product or using a similar process run on that line now, and can I speak to them?General references don’t help. You want someone running your kind of process, such as hot-fill glass, pouches, or aseptic cartons. If they have never run your format, your first batch is their trial.Contact details for someone at a brand with a similar line.
3Do you require a pilot run before full production?A factory willing to jump straight into a large commercial run on a new formula, without scaling up first, is skipping basic engineering discipline.A small trial run first, to check yields, hold times, and line speeds.

Will you matter to them?

4How full is your plant, is your business seasonal, and what share of your volume would my account be?If you are a tiny part of a plant’s volume, your run can get bumped when a bigger customer needs line time. If you would be most of its volume, it may lack the capacity or backup to grow with you.Honest figures on how full the plant is, when its busy seasons fall, and where you would sit. There’s no universal ideal, so ask several plants and compare.
5What is your minimum run size, and does it change for a first order?The minimum run size is the smallest batch they will make. It decides how much stock and cash you commit on every run.A clear commercial minimum, in writing.

The money

6What are your payment terms for a new account, and how do you bill materials and line time?Payment terms show how healthy the plant’s cash flow is. A factory that wants everything up front, before it buys materials, may be using your money to cover its own costs.Terms in writing, for example a deposit to buy materials and a balance after the batch is made and checked.
7Will you buy the ingredients and packaging, or do we supply them? What do you charge to receive and store what we supply? (This is turnkey versus tolling.)Brands that supply their own ingredients or packaging film often get surprise receiving and storage fees.Written rules on when materials must arrive, how long storage is free, and what each pallet costs after that.
8How much waste do you allow on ingredients and packaging, and who pays beyond that?Filling and labeling machines can burn through a lot of custom packaging during setup. Without an agreed limit, your packaging becomes the plant’s free scrap.A stated waste allowance, with anything above it credited back on the invoice.

Quality and proof

9Which food safety certifications does this specific plant hold, and can I see the latest audit summary? (See food safety certification.)“We follow SQF standards” is not the same as holding the certificate. If a retailer you want to sell to requires certification, an uncertified plant can stop your expansion.A current certificate for that exact site, with the audit result. Your regulatory advisor can confirm what your market requires.
10Will you sign our product spec and initial each critical range?Problems should come up on paper before a run, not in front of an audience after it.Yes, with ranges you have both agreed in writing.
11Who pulls the samples for inspection, your team or ours?A sample the factory picked only tells you about the units it wanted you to see.The inspector chooses the units. Plant staff only move pallets and cases.
12Can an inspector working for us be on the floor, including at startup?A plant that hesitates to let your inspector on the floor may intend for you to take whatever it hands you.Open access, with a normal safety orientation and short advance notice.
13Will you send a certificate of analysis with every lot, and accept it being compared with independent readings?The certificate is the factory’s own claim. Independent readings show whether it holds.Yes, without hesitation.
14How is a disputed reading re-tested, by which accredited lab, and who pays?The lab’s result should settle a dispute. Decide the lab and the cost before the first lot, not after a failure.A named accredited lab and a clear answer on who pays, written into the contract.
15What happens to a failed lot, and by when must you submit a corrective action?Without a deadline, a failed lot turns into a negotiation.A written process, with a deadline, for what the factory will fix and when.
16Can you change an ingredient, supplier, or process step without our written sign -off? (Formally, this is an engineering change notice.)Plants sometimes adjust line speeds or swap suppliers to improve their own output, and shelf life or mouthfeel can change without anyone intending it.Any change to process, hold time, or ingredient supplier needs your written approval first.
17Is final payment tied to a passed pre-shipment inspection?A payment tied to a result protects you in a way a payment tied to a date can’t.The balance is released after inspection, and the terms are written into the contract.
18Who carries the loss on a rejected lot once it leaves the dock?Shipping terms (see Incoterms) decide who bears the loss, so confirm them before you sign.Terms your lawyer has read, and that you understand.

If a co-packer pushes back on the first few questions about quality, that tells you how the first off-spec lot will go. Your counsel should word the answers into the agreement. This article is general information, not legal advice.
